Time Study & BasicMOST Analysis

Setting a standard time for an Arduino enclosure I designed and 3D-printed, then checking it against a predetermined-time estimate.

Time studyBasicMOSTPerformance ratingAllowancesExcelFusion 3603D printing

At a glance

Problem

How long should one enclosure take to assemble? I needed a fair standard time for a six-step manual station where the operator sets the housing, seats an Arduino, fits the cover and fastens four nuts.

What I did

Designed and 3D-printed the enclosure, timed 20 cycles of a volunteer operator, rated each element, added 13% allowances, and checked the result against BasicMOST.

Result

A standard time of 108.7 s, about 33 assemblies an hour. Starting the nuts was half the cycle; a nut tray, a powered driver or threaded inserts could bring it toward 80 s.

The full analysis

Role
Designed the product and ran the study
Methods
Stopwatch time study and BasicMOST (IE 3123 Industrial Ergonomics)
Tools
Fusion 360, 3D printer, Excel
Date
2026

The station

I designed a two-part enclosure for an Arduino Uno in Fusion 360 and 3D-printed it. It has a housing with four locating pins for the board and a vented cover that drops over four studs and is held down with four nuts. The station is one seated operator with every part within reach.

How I timed it

A volunteer operator assembled 20 enclosures while I timed six elements with a stopwatch, reading the clock at each element's end point and resetting it at the start of every cycle. I noted anything unusual, like a dropped nut or the board coming off its pins.

Cycle times fell from an average of 116.3 s over the first ten cycles to 95.8 s over the last ten, about 18% faster, and flattened out around cycle 11. A standard should describe someone who already knows the job, so I used cycles 11 to 20 only. Two readings in that window were left out because something went wrong; the allowance already covers that kind of lost time. The remaining cycles were consistent, with a 3.1% coefficient of variation and a 95% confidence interval of ±2.5 s on the cycle mean.

Rating and allowances

I rated each element with the Westinghouse system (skill, effort, conditions and consistency) and wrote down what I saw to justify each grade. Ratings ran from 0.98 for starting the nuts, where the operator fumbled for the threads, to 1.06 for the simple moves, for 1.01 overall.

Allowances add up to 13%. Personal time is 5%. Fatigue is 8%; the ILO points system gave 15 points, or 7%, and I raised it a point for the repeated finger work of spinning four nuts by hand. Delay is 0% because nothing at the station makes the operator wait.

Result

The standard time came out to 108.7 seconds, or about 33 assemblies an hour. Starting the four nuts accounts for half of it.

Checking it with BasicMOST

I coded the same six elements in BasicMOST, a predetermined-time system that builds a time from standard motion values. It predicts 51.4 s of normal time against the stopwatch's 96.2 s, which is 47% lower. It came in lower on all six elements, so the difference is spread across the whole task.

I see three likely reasons. BasicMOST assumes a fully trained operator, and mine had about 20 cycles of practice. My ratings were by eye with no benchmark to compare against. And my sequences leave out motions I saw, like regrasping parts and feeling for threads. Running the nuts down matched best, which makes sense because steady finger spinning is the kind of motion MOST models well.

Where the time goes

Starting the nuts took 48.7 s against MOST's 23.0 s, about 12 seconds per nut instead of 6. The nuts sat in a loose pile, so picking up just one often took more than one try, and each thread had to be found by feel.

Recommendations

  • Add a nut tray or dispenser (low effort). It spreads the nuts out or feeds them one at a time, so each grab picks up exactly one nut, and it costs very little.
  • Use a powered socket driver (medium effort). It starts and runs down each nut in one motion, which turns Elements 4 and 5 into a single step and removes the hand spinning.
  • Build the threads into the enclosure (high effort). This is a design change, with threaded inserts in the housing and screws holding the cover down. Without loose nuts to pick up and start, most of Element 4 goes away.

If starting the nuts came all the way down to the BasicMOST estimate, the standard would drop from 108.7 s to about 80 s, roughly 45 assemblies an hour instead of 33. That assumes the full improvement, so treat it as the most this could save.

What I learned

Most of the extra time went to handling the parts, like picking one nut out of a loose pile and finding its thread. Changing how the nuts are set out at the station would probably save more time than asking the operator to work faster.
