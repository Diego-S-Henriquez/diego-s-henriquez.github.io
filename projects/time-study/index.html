Time Study & BasicMOST Analysis

Setting a standard time for an Arduino enclosure I designed and 3D-printed, then checking it against a predetermined-time estimate.

Time studyBasicMOSTPerformance ratingAllowancesExcelFusion 3603D printing

At a glance

Problem

How long should one enclosure take to assemble? I needed a fair standard time for a six-step manual station: set the housing, seat an Arduino, fit the cover and fasten four nuts.

What I did

Designed and 3D-printed the enclosure, timed 20 cycles of a volunteer operator, rated each element, added 13% allowances, and checked the result against BasicMOST.

Result

A standard time of 108.7 s, about 33 assemblies an hour. Starting the nuts was half the cycle; a nut tray, a powered driver or threaded inserts could bring it toward 80 s.

The full analysis

The station

I designed a two-part enclosure for an Arduino Uno in Fusion 360 and 3D-printed it: a housing with four locating pins for the board, and a vented cover that drops over four studs and is held down with four nuts. The station is one seated operator with every part within reach.

How I timed it

A volunteer operator assembled 20 enclosures while I timed six elements with a stopwatch, reading the clock at each element's end point and resetting it at the start of every cycle. I noted anything unusual, like a dropped nut or the board coming off its pins.

Cycle times fell from an average of 116.3 s over the first ten cycles to 95.8 s over the last ten, about 18% faster, and flattened out around cycle 11. A standard should describe someone who already knows the job, so I used cycles 11 to 20 only. Two readings in that window were left out because something went wrong; the allowance already covers that kind of lost time. What remained was consistent: a 3.1% coefficient of variation, with a 95% confidence interval of ±2.5 s on the cycle mean.

Rating and allowances

I rated each element with the Westinghouse system (skill, effort, conditions and consistency) and wrote down what I saw to justify each grade. Ratings ran from 0.98 for starting the nuts, where the operator fumbled for the threads, to 1.06 for the simple moves, for 1.01 overall.

Allowances total 13%: 5% personal; 8% fatigue (15 points on the ILO table, or 7%, raised to 8% for the repeated finger work of spinning four nuts by hand); and 0% delay, since nothing makes the operator wait.

Result

A standard time of 108.7 seconds, or about 33 assemblies an hour. Starting the four nuts is half of it on its own.

Checking it with BasicMOST

I coded the same six elements in BasicMOST, a predetermined-time system that builds a time from standard motion values. It predicts 51.4 s of normal time against the stopwatch's 96.2 s: 47% lower, and lower on every element, so the gap isn't one miscoded step.

The likely reasons: BasicMOST assumes a fully trained operator, and mine had about 20 cycles of practice; my ratings were by eye with no benchmark; and my sequences leave out motions I saw, like regrasping parts and feeling for threads. Running the nuts down matched best, which fits: steady finger spinning is the kind of motion MOST models well.

Where the time goes

Starting the nuts took 48.7 s against MOST's 23.0 s, about 12 seconds per nut instead of 6. The nuts sat in a loose pile, so picking up just one often took more than one try, and each thread had to be found by feel.

Recommendations

  • A nut tray or dispenser. Low effort Spreads the nuts out, or feeds them one at a time, so each grab gets exactly one nut. It fixes the loose-pile problem for very little cost.
  • A powered socket driver. Medium effort Starts and runs down each nut in one motion, so Elements 4 and 5 become a single step, with no finding threads by feel or spinning by hand.
  • Threads built into the enclosure. High effort A design change: threaded inserts in the housing and screws to hold the cover. With no loose nuts to pick up and start, most of Element 4 goes away.

If starting the nuts came all the way down to the BasicMOST estimate, the standard would drop from 108.7 s to about 80 s, roughly 45 assemblies an hour instead of 33. That's an upper limit on the savings.

What I learned

The slowest part of the job wasn't the motion itself but the handling around it: grabbing one nut from a pile and finding its thread. How parts are presented at a station can matter more than how fast someone works.
